Power · stability · strength

How the strength work is built

Three short sessions a week. Two are leg & power days periodised to match the running: foundation (tissue, single-leg control, core) → max strength (heavy force, the engine behind economy) → power (plyometrics and fast lifts that raise your cadence) → taper off before race day. The third is a back & upper-body day, built around your back. Day A pairs with Tuesday's quality run so hard days stay hard; Day B sits on Friday, clear of the Sunday long run; Day C (back & upper) goes on Thursday, your easy-run day. Lift after you run on shared days, and never load the legs heavily the day before a long run.

The back day, specifically. It leads with back extensions — isolated lumbar-extensor training has twenty-plus years of evidence for building back strength and easing low-back pain (Steele, Fisher et al., Solent lumbar-extension research) — then rows, pulls, dead hangs and loaded carries for posture and a decompressed spine, in the spirit of the kneesovertoes (ATG) approach. One honest caveat: I can't assess your back, and the right moves depend on what's actually going on with it. Build every back movement up from bodyweight, stop anything that triggers sharp or one-sided pain, and if the issue is ongoing get a physio's eyes on it before loading — especially before trying loaded spinal flexion like the Jefferson curl, which helps some backs and aggravates others.

Why strength at all: it's one of the few interventions shown to improve distance-running economy — same pace, lower effort — without adding a kilometre (Blagrove et al., 2018 systematic review), and for a newer runner it's the best defence against overuse injury.

Prescriptions assume a barbell/dumbbells and a pull-up bar. Minimal setup — bands, a kettlebell, bodyweight — tell me and I'll swap in equivalents. Three strength days on top of four runs is a full week from a 5K base; if it's too much, the back & upper day is the one to shorten or move, not drop the easy running.

Race day

Pacing for 24 January

Even effort, flat course

  • Don't chase the gun. The first km feels easy on purpose — let faster runners go.
  • 0–5 km: settle at 5:38–5:40, find your breathing.
  • 5–16 km: lock in 5:33–5:36 and hold. This is the race.
  • 16–21 km: spend whatever's left. If it's there, drop to 5:25.
  • Fuel: one gel around 45–60 min, sip water at aid stations. Rehearse it on long runs from Week 9.
  • Coast in January: usually mild, often a sea breeze — tuck behind a group into any headwind.

Hold the whole thing together

Five rules that matter more than any session

  • Easy means easy. Roughly 80% of your running should be conversational. Keeping easy days truly easy is what lets the hard days land and keeps you off the injury list.
  • Strength 2–3× a week, including a back day. Built into every week above — open a session in the cards to see the moves and log your sets. For someone ramping from a 5K base it's your biggest injury insurance.
  • The niggle rule. A sharp, one-sided pain that changes how you run means stop. Two or three easy days rarely costs fitness; running through an injury can cost the race.
  • Consistency beats any single workout. Miss one? Don't cram it in — just rejoin the plan where it is.
  • Sleep and food fuel the training, not just race day. The adaptation happens while you recover.
